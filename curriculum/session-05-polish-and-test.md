# Session 5: Polish and test — Hand it over, watch closely

**Goal:** every teen leaves with (1) their site tested by someone else and the top 2 problems fixed, (2) a site that works on a phone, (3) a README that has "AI credits", and (4) a plan for a 2–3 minute demo. They also form a view on who owns AI-written code, and on what the AI couldn't do for them.

**Deck:** [`slideshows/session-05-polish-and-test.pptx`](../slideshows/session-05-polish-and-test.pptx). The speaker notes on each slide hold the script.

## Before the session

- [ ] Print the silent-test note sheets (3 columns, as on slide 5), 2 per teen.
- [ ] Make signs for the agree/disagree activity: tape **AGREE** on one wall, **DISAGREE** on the opposite wall, and **NOT SURE** in the middle. Check with library staff that the room can be used this way.
- [ ] Share the README template from slide 18 somewhere teens can copy it.
- [ ] Send families a demo day invite, with the date, the time and the room.
- [ ] Decide the demo order for session 6 and how long each demo gets. With 15–20 teens at 3 minutes each, demos take about 60 minutes.

## Run of show (120 min)

| Time | Slides | Block | Who leads |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:08 | 1–2 | **Check-in.** Teens share what happened when someone outside the workshop tried their site (last week's homework). | Leader A |
| 0:08–0:35 | 3–6 | **Silent user testing.** Two rounds, each with a new partner from a different table. The tester thinks out loud and the builder stays silent and takes notes. Then each teen picks their top 2 fixes. | Leader B keeps time |
| 0:35–0:57 | 7–14 | **AI Checkpoint: Who owns it?** Teens stand by the wall for agree, disagree or not sure as each of 4 statements comes up. Then what we know about ownership, and what the AI couldn't do. | Leader A runs it, Leader B keeps the discussion moving |

| 1:35–1:50 | 18 | **README with AI credits.** | Both leaders circulate |
| 1:50–2:00 | 19–20 | Demo day format and homework | Leader B |

## Notes on the ownership facts (slide 13)

Keep this light. **It's not legal advice, and the rules are still changing.** What's reasonably settled for a teen audience:

- AI companies' terms generally say the user owns the output. Check the terms of the specific tool before stating this.
- The US Copyright Office's position, from its 2025 report on copyright and AI, is that material generated purely by AI isn't protected by copyright. Human contributions, such as your own writing and code, meaningful changes, and creative selection and arrangement, can be.
- AI can reproduce code that is close to other people's existing code. That's why we credit what the AI did, and why the teens' own changes matter.

The goal isn't legal precision. It's the habit of saying honestly what the AI did, which is exactly what the README's AI credits section is for.

## If things go wrong

- **A tester gets stuck and the builder can't help jumping in:** the leader says "Freeze! Write it down." The moment someone gets confused is the most useful data in the test.
- **The agree/disagree activity gets heated:** fine. Make sure both sides talk, and keep it about ideas, not people. A teen who doesn't want to move can say "not sure" from their seat.
- **The site is broken the week before demo day:** pair the teen with a junior mentor. The goal is that the Must feature works live. A Nice feature can be cut.
- **A teen wants to add a big new feature:** no. This week is polish only. New ideas go on the Someday list and into the "what's next" part of the demo.

## Measure

- Attendance
- How many teens completed a silent test and fixed at least one issue from it
- How many teens have a README with AI credits
- Agree/disagree counts for each statement, if you want before-and-after data on attitudes
